## The problem started with a good thing

I'm a master's student in Applied Artificial Intelligence, which is a fancy way of saying I have a professional excuse to try every tool that shows up on my radar. A few days ago that tool was [NotebookLM](https://notebooklm.google.com). Some friends from uni swore by it for studying, and since we're deep in exam-prep season, I figured I'd give it a real shot.

I get the hype now. The feature that won me over is the one that takes your uploaded sources and turns them into a ~30-minute podcast, two hosts casually discussing the material. It's not a replacement for the deep dive, but as a *warm-up* it's excellent. Before I sit down and grind through a topic, I get a relaxed overview of what it's about, why it matters, and how it connects to everything else. Those little bits of edge context are exactly the thing that usually takes me an hour of scattered reading to assemble on my own.

So I started generating podcasts for my exam topics. Great. Loved it.

And then I had to actually *listen* to them.

## The real problem: the default player is rough

I dropped the audio files onto my iPhone and opened them in Apple's built-in player, and that's where the good vibes ended. A couple of things drove me up the wall:

- I'd switch apps for one second — check a message, glance at a PDF — and come back to find it had **forgotten how far into the recording I was**. For a 30-minute episode you're half-listening to while reading, that's brutal.
- The controls are just... not built for this. No **skip back 10 seconds**, no **skip forward 10 seconds**. When a host says something dense and you want to rewind five seconds to catch it, you're stuck fumbling with a scrubber.

None of this is a huge deal in isolation. But when it happens fifteen times an evening, while you're already stressed about exams, it stops being a minor annoyance and starts being *the* annoyance.

## The fix: build the thing

Here's the part I'm a little proud of. Instead of hunting for yet another podcast app and hoping it did local files well, I decided to just build exactly what I wanted.

While I was studying, I had a terminal running quietly in the background with **Claude Code**, loaded up with some genuinely great skills — [superpowers](https://github.com/obra/superpowers) among them. I opened up **Google Stitch**, quickly sketched a mockup of the interface I had in my head, and handed that mockup over as the design target.

The brief was simple: a local, Spotify-style audio player where I can store my own files and organize my AI-generated podcasts for exam prep. I called it **Podify** — as in, podcasts.

Then I let it run.

## What "let it run" actually looked like

Claude Code went for almost **seven hours**. It didn't just spit out a file and call it done — it worked in a loop: writing code, automatically testing it, reviewing its own work, and iterating. It built the whole thing in **Swift**, using **SwiftData** for persistence.

I want to be honest about the vibe here, because it's easy to make this sound more magical than it was. I wasn't babysitting every line. I checked in between study blocks, glanced at what it was doing, and mostly let it cook while I focused on the material I was supposed to be learning in the first place. The fact that it self-tested and self-reviewed is a big part of why I could do that — I wasn't the bottleneck.

## The result

What I ended up with is a local audio player that is, unironically, *nice*:

- It **remembers exactly where I left off**, even after I've switched apps and come back. The dashboard even greets me with how much time is left on whatever I was in the middle of.
- It has the controls I actually wanted — including the **10-second skip forward and back** buttons that started this whole thing.
- I can **organize my podcasts** properly, sort them into a library, and treat my little collection of NotebookLM episodes like a real archive instead of a pile of loose audio files.

It's dark-themed, it's got a clean now-playing screen with a big satisfying play button, and a home dashboard that says "Welcome back, Curator." It looks like something I'd have been happy to download from the App Store — except it does precisely the one job I needed and nothing else.

## What I actually take away from this

The obvious lesson is "AI tools are getting scary good," and sure, fine. But that's not really the part that stuck with me.

What stuck with me is how **short the distance from annoyance to solution** has gotten. A year or two ago, "the default player is bad" would have ended with me grumbling and adjusting my behavior around the tool. This time, the annoyance was specific enough and the tooling was capable enough that building the exact fix became the *easier* path than tolerating the problem. That's a genuinely new feeling.

I also think there's something healthy about the fact that the project was small and *personal*. I didn't build a startup. I built a thing for an audience of one — me, during exam season — with requirements I understood perfectly because I was the one being annoyed. Those are the best side projects. No product-market fit to worry about. The market is me, and I am extremely satisfied.

Now, if you'll excuse me, I have a 25-minute podcast about the semantics of arithmetic in Late Latin to get back to — and this time the player will remember where I was.
