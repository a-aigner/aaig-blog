## Why this exists

The suite is two products for one small firm: replai, the e-mail assistant,
and a chat that runs next to it. Both need to know who's allowed to do what.
And "allowed" turns out to mean at least eight different things. May this
person sign in at all? Use the chat? Run the administration screens? Run the
*chat's* administration? Read the firm's shared `info@` mailbox? Search the CRM
from the chat? Change which AI models the chat uses? See how often colleagues
use the product?

In a company with an IT department, each of those has an owner who knows
where it's set. Here there's nobody, so I held to one rule: **every answer
lives in exactly one place, and the screen tells you which place that is.**
This article goes through the answers, and through the administration console
where most of them are visible.

## One application, not two

I'll start with a decision about the console itself, because it shapes
everything below. It would have been natural to build administration as its
own separate portal. The design notes say why I didn't: a separate application
"would need its own authentication, its own build and its own place in
`compose.yaml`'s bind table, and every one of those is a second copy of
something that already works."

So the console is a section of replai, under `/console`, reachable only with
its own permission. Same address, same login, same design. That last part
matters more to me than it might sound. The console uses the same warm paper
background, the same serif type and the same brass accent as the rest of the
product, so an administrator never feels they've been dropped into a
different, scarier tool. It *is* the same tool, with more responsibility.

Two habits run through every console screen, and they're design decisions, not
decoration. First, **each screen opens by saying what it can't do.** The People screen says, before anything else, that passwords stay in the
directory and that "this screen never sets one."
Second, **states are written as words, not colours.** "Suspended." "Recorded,
not yet in force." "Not changeable here." A colour tells you something is
different. Only a word tells you what.

## Step one: the directory decides who you are

Both products ask the same **directory**, Keycloak, who someone is. Engineers
call such a directory a *realm*. Inside it, people sit in **groups**, which are
like folders, and groups carry **roles**, which are named permissions a product
checks. Put someone in a group and they get its roles.

There are three groups. `chat-users` may use the chat. `replai-users` may use
the e-mail assistant. And `suite-admins` are the people who run the thing.
Membership of `suite-admins` is the single switch for administration in *both*
products at once, and taking someone out undoes both.

Neither product keeps its own password. replai's login form was removed in
August. The chat's local login, registration and password-reset routes were
removed too, not switched off, and the chat refuses to start if a setting would
bring them back. One door, and it's the directory's.

Group membership is copied into replai at every sign-in, and it's *replaced*,
not added to. That sounds like a detail. It's the difference between "leaving
a department revokes access" and "leaving a department changes nothing, quietly,
forever". A background job also checks the directory every fifteen minutes, so
a deactivated person loses access within that window, which is the figure the
customer's data protection officer was given.

The People screen is where an administrator does this without ever seeing
Keycloak. They can add someone, tick their groups, suspend them and restore
them. There's no password field and, deliberately, **no Delete button**.
Removing someone from the directory doesn't delete what replai stores about
them, and the screen says so in full. What's kept and for how long is the
firm's decision under its deletion policy, not a button's side effect.

And next to the Suspend button, the screen lists the shared mailboxes that
person reads, with a sentence: their correspondence "belongs to the firm and
remains when this account is suspended." Without that line, "suspend" reads as
"and their mail goes too". An administrator who believes that might delete the
mailbox to be thorough, and that mistake can't be undone.

## Step two: the chat administrator nobody appointed

On 26 September I asked the running system a simple question: who is an
administrator in the chat? The answer was exactly one account. **The console's
own robot account**, made administrator by a database update I'd written by
hand to get the console working. No person at the firm was one. None could become one, either.

The chat can read administrator status from the directory, but only if three
settings are all present. With two of three it silently behaves as if none were
set. Now all three are required, and the chat refuses to start without them.
Administration comes from the realm role `chat-admin`, which the `suite-admins`
group carries. The chat's own "make this person an admin" routes are gone.
They answer "not found", not "forbidden", because they don't exist anymore.

There was a second half to it. The chat keeps trusting a sign-in until its
refresh token expires. Upstream's default was **seven days**, so an
administrator removed on Monday could stay administrator until the following
Monday. It's now eight hours, one working day, and the chat refuses to start
with anything over twenty-four.

## Step three: a mailbox membership is a permission

The firm's shared mailboxes, `info@` and the like, have members, and being a
member is what lets someone read it.

Membership is always explicit. I could have made shared mail readable by the
whole firm, and rejected it in one line: `info@` is correspondence, and
deciding that everyone may read it would be making a privacy decision on the
customer's behalf.

Asking for mail you're not allowed to see gets a **404, "not found"**, never a
403, "forbidden". The difference is small and it matters. "You may not read
this thread" confirms that the thread exists, and that alone says who the firm
is writing to. "Not found" says nothing. The chat had the same leak on
conversations, telling you "forbidden" for someone else's and "not found" for
a missing one, and got the same fix.

Every change to a membership is recorded with who made it, and the name is
kept as text, so the record outlives the administrator's own account. Look at
the third member in the screenshot, "added by an earlier administrator".

## Step four: what nobody can change from inside

Some settings shouldn't be changeable by anyone clicking around, administrator
included. Which AI models the chat may use. How long conversations are kept.
Which tools the model is offered. How people sign in. Those are fixed when the
software is deployed, and the product says so.

This screen existed as a promise before it was true. The chat's own admin interface could override several of these settings per
role, group or person, the model runtimes, the retention and the tool list
among them, skipping every check the chat runs at startup. An override could have brought
back the eleven cloud tools the [companion article](/projects/replai/fifty-green-tests-and-a-server-that-could-not-start)
removed. Those overrides are now refused when someone tries to save one, and
dropped when older saved settings are loaded.

Each row shows the value the chat service *reports it is running with*, not
what the file says. They should match. If they ever don't, this is where you'd
see it.

## Two permissions that are about restraint

Two last decisions are permissions in the other direction, limits on what even
an administrator can see.

Firm-wide usage figures, how many drafts were accepted and how much people
edited them, exist so someone can decide whether the product earns its place.
They must never become a way to watch one employee. So no setting selects a
person, the period is one of three fixed lengths, and a figure is withheld when
fewer than five people are behind it. A deployment can raise that number. The
code won't let it go below two.

And for the day the directory itself is down, there's an emergency way in. It
isn't a switch that stays on. Someone with access to the server itself creates
a one-time link that expires after thirty minutes, and both creating and using
it are recorded. A flag left on after an incident looks exactly like one that
was never set. An expired link can't be left on.

## Where it stands

The sign-in groundwork has been in the product since August. The console
screens, the chat's administrator role and the fixed settings are on a working
branch, finished and tested, not yet merged. The screens in this article are the real ones from that branch,
rendered with sample people and a sample firm. None of it is a separate admin tool. After writing this, I'm more convinced it
shouldn't be. Every permission question above got easier to answer because there was
only one product asking it.
