## Why this exists

The firm already had its knowledge indexed. Fifteen years of CRM history sit
in a vector database called Qdrant, put there by the pipeline from the first
part of this series, and the e-mail assistant searches it whenever it drafts a
reply. The chat product, a few containers away on the same machine,
couldn't see any of it.

Connecting the two is technically a small job. Doing it without opening a door
you didn't mean to open is most of the work. This article walks through the
bridge the way an administrator and then an employee meet it, one screen at a
time. The [companion article](/projects/replai/fifty-green-tests-and-a-server-that-could-not-start)
has the bugs. This one has the design.

A few words first, since they come up all the way through. A **vector
database** stores pieces of text together with a list of numbers that places
each piece by what it means, so a question can find passages that mean
something similar even when the words differ. A **collection** is one such
named store. And **MCP**, the Model Context Protocol, is a standard way for a
chat model to call an outside tool: the model says "run *search* with this
text", the chat runs it, and the model reads the answer before replying.

## Step one: a collection becomes a source, once someone accepts what that means

Everything starts on a settings screen in replai, where an administrator
registers a Qdrant collection. Address, collection name, which field holds the
readable text, which one holds a title. Two buttons do the checking, and both
say precisely what they check. **Test connection** "dials what is stored, not
what is typed above", so you save first and then test the thing that will
actually run. **Read schema** looks at one stored point to see its shape, and
the screen is careful about what that proves: "One point is evidence, not a
contract."

Then comes the field that decides everything, the **company key**. For the
e-mail assistant, every source should be *scoped*: a draft to one client must
only ever find that client's records. A source without a company key isn't
scoped, and the screen won't let that pass silently.

Ticking "I understand, and want this source enabled anyway" is a recorded
decision. The name of the person who ticked it and the date are stored and
shown, as plain text, so the record survives even if that account is deleted
later. And the second warning is the reason this matters for the chat: an
unscoped source is **the only kind that can reach it**. A chat question isn't a letter to one client. There's nothing to scope it to. Either the
whole firm may know it, and someone said so on this screen, or the chat never
sees it.

Unticking works the other way, and immediately. The screen says so before you
save: the chat "stops being offered this source immediately", without waiting for a
restart. That's not a promise the interface makes on hope. The list of
offerable sources is worked out fresh on every single request, which costs
about five milliseconds.

## Step two: a source becomes a server that knows nobody

Between the chat and the collection sits a small service I'll call the corpus
server. For every source it offers exactly one address, and at that address
exactly one tool, `search`. The tool takes a question and an optional number
of results. It deliberately takes **no argument saying which source to
search**. Which source is fixed by the address the chat was configured with.
So a model can't talk its way into a collection it wasn't handed, because the
choice was never offered to it.

The corpus server knows no users at all. It has no login, no token and no
published port. Only the chat's container can reach it, over the internal
network, and the code says why that's enough: "A token here would protect
nothing the network does not." All decisions about *people* happen on the
chat's side of the line. The corpus server just answers questions about text,
and it can't name a person's mailbox or personal documents even if asked,
which is checked by a test that reads its code.

Not every source searches the same way. Qdrant collections are searched by
meaning, which needs the embedding model running. The firm's plain document
store is searched by words, with the database's full-text search, so it keeps
answering even when the model containers are down.

## Step three: the console decides who may ask

Knowing that a source *may* be offered to the chat still isn't the same as
deciding *who* gets it. That happens in the administration console, on a
screen that lists every source the chat could be given.

Each source gets a switch and a grid of people. That's the whole interface,
and most of the design is in the words next to it. A row doesn't just say on
or off. It says **"In force in the chat"** when the chat has confirmed it,
**"Recorded, not yet in force"** when the console has written the decision down
but the chat hasn't caught up, and it names the error in plain words when
writing failed. A decision and its effect are different things, and a screen
that shows them as one is how an administrator ends up certain of something
that isn't true.

Someone who has never signed in to the chat is shown, greyed out, with "(never
signed in)". The chat doesn't know them yet, so there's nothing to grant them
access *to*. Hiding them would look like a bug. Showing them as tickable would
be a lie.

Why one server per source instead of one server with everything? Because the
chat's own permission system works per server. Each server has its own list of
people allowed to use it, and the chat enforces that list itself. Splitting
the sources is what lets a per-person decision in replai become a per-person
rule inside the chat.

Getting it there is the job of a **reconciler**, a routine that compares what
the console decided with what the chat actually has and fixes the difference.
It runs straight after every change and again every hour. It writes the
complete list of allowed people each time, not just the changes, so running it
twice does no harm. And there's one thing it will never do: delete a server it
didn't create. Those get listed under their own heading, "In the chat, but not
configured here", because somebody may have added them on purpose.

## Step four: what the person in the chat sees

For the employee, most of this is invisible, which is right. A source they
weren't granted simply isn't there. The model doesn't see it, can't call it,
and nobody gets an awkward "access denied" in the middle of a conversation.
It also can't be reached by the back door. Someone who was refused a source
can't get at it through an assistant a colleague shared with them either. The
chat drops that tool when it assembles the person's tools, and refuses the
call a second time where every tool call passes, and writes the refusal down.

When a question needs a lookup, the model picks the search tool and the chat
stops before running it. An approval card appears with the tool's name, the
exact text that will be searched for, and four buttons. Genehmigen, Ablehnen,
Bearbeiten, Antworten. The person approves, edits, or declines. An approval
that nobody gives expires after one hour.

What comes back is deliberately plain. Up to eight passages, each carrying the
source it came from and the text, and nothing else. No scores, no internal ids.
In the first full test, the question was about a particular ticket. The model
asked to search for it, I approved, the passages came back, and the model
answered in German from them, noting that the test data was placeholder text
instead of inventing something more convincing. That last part is the whole
point of grounding a model in documents.

And when a store can't be reached, the tool doesn't answer with an empty list.
It fails, with the words "nothing was read, so nothing was found". An empty
list would let the model tell someone, confidently, that the firm has no record
of something it simply couldn't look at.

## What each search leaves behind

Every call to a knowledge source writes one line to the chat's audit log, a
log that can only be added to, where each entry is chained to the one before
so a deleted line would show. The line records who searched, which source,
the outcome and how long it took. It deliberately does **not** record the
question or the answer. It can show that the CRM was searched on Tuesday
afternoon by someone in accounts. It can't show what for, and I think that's
the right amount to know about a colleague.

The whole search, measured on a small test collection of a few hundred
tickets, takes about 38 milliseconds without the reranker. Turning the question
into numbers is more than half of that. The reranker, a second model that
reads each candidate against the question, adds about 300 milliseconds, and in
three test questions it changed which passages came back every time.

## The trade-off I wrote down

One decision here isn't free, and it's in the compliance documents in so many
words. For the console to tell the chat who may use which server, it has to
talk to the chat's own administration interface, and the only way the chat
accepts that is with a token signed by the chat's own secret key. That key
signs every user's session. So anything that can read replai's configuration
could, in principle, sign a session for anyone in the chat. The customer chose
that on 24 September over the alternatives, and the documentation says exactly
what it means. I'd rather have a disclosed wide credential than a hidden one.
